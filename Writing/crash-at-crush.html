Texas History

The Crash at Crush

By Karen Hodges

★

In 1896, a railroad publicity spectacle drew tens of thousands of people to a temporary Texas town—and ended in catastrophe.

A Texas spectacle remembered

A faint roar was heard at first. Soon, the sound grew louder, like an oncoming tornado. Two trains hurtled toward one another on the same track. The engineers leaped from the doomed trains as the iron monsters approached each other, moving between 50 and 90 mph. At the moment of impact, the engines reared like fighting monsters as the boxcars stacked on one another and broke into millions of pieces. Between 40,000 and 50,000 people watched this planned crash, which turned into an unplanned disaster.

In 1896, Willie Crush was a young mustached agent for the Missouri, Kansas, and Texas Railroad, nicknamed the “Katy.” He was looking for a way to increase the number of persons who rode the train and to encourage farmers to use the train to carry their farm products and livestock to market. He heard about a wreck on a railroad line near Columbus, Ohio, that had attracted a crowd of 40,000. Willie believed he could make the Katy a household word by planning a collision between two trains and inviting the world to view the spectacle.

Willie picked a piece of land near the small Czech community of West, Texas, south of Waco, along the Katy tracks. He named it “Crush, Texas” after himself. Then he advertised the upcoming crash from Kansas to the Gulf Coast of Texas.

He chose two twenty-seven-year-old Baldwin locomotives with good diamond stacks. Number 999 was painted green with red trim, and Number 1001 was painted red with green trim. Each of these locomotives weighed thirty-five tons (seventy thousand pounds). He strung boxcars behind each one and painted advertisements for the old Oriental Hotel in Dallas and the Ringling Brothers Circus on them. He chose young, agile men as engineers who would be able to jump from the machines once they were rolling and their throttles were tied down.

The rails would be cut behind the doomed trains to prevent a runaway if either train jumped the track. Willie was concerned that the boilers, the machines that produced the steam for the trains to run, would explode in the collision. All but one of the engineers assured Willie that this would not happen. However, a veteran engineer from Ireland named Mr. Hanrahan believed the boilers would surely blow up. For safety, Willie decided that he would keep the spectators one hundred feet from the tracks. Only the press and Katy officials would view from a closer distance.

The day of the crash, Sept. 15, 1896, was warm and clear. Thirty-three special trains arrived from as far away as Kansas and Chicago, carrying eager spectators. Many others came on foot or by wagon. Willie wanted the people to enjoy themselves. He ordered eight tank cars of water with plenty of tin cups. There were a dozen lemonade stands, cigar stands, and carnival games. A restaurant was set up under a big tent borrowed from Ringling Brothers Circus, which served kraut and sausages. Even a wooden jail was erected to house thieves or pickpockets who might be drawn to the event.

The crowd filled the hillside, sitting on observation platforms, perching in trees, and standing on wagon beds. At around 5:00 p.m., the engineers drove the trains nose to nose to “kiss” cowcatchers, so the crowd could see the two trains up close. The spectators were so excited they didn’t want to move back safely from the tracks. After an hour, Willie threatened to cancel the event in order to convince them to return to the viewing area. The engines were put in reverse and backed up the slope about two miles apart.

“Willie lifted his hat and dropped it, the signal for the trains to roll.”

Mounted on a white horse, Willie paraded before the crowd. Each of the engineers signaled with two sharp whistles that they were ready. Willie lifted his hat and dropped it, the signal for the trains to roll. The engineers blew the train whistles, and hundreds of cherry bombs went off along the tracks as the trains roared toward one another. After they collided, the crowd heard three explosions. One explosion was the sound of the trains crashing together, and the other two were each of the boilers exploding. Hot metal, pieces of wood, and steam flew up into the air and then down on the terrified crowd. Willie sadly realized that Mr. Hanrahan had been correct about the boilers.

No one knew which direction to run to avoid the falling debris. Some people were injured by the flying metal and wood, while some were burned by the steam from the boilers. The official photographer, Jervis Deane, lost an eye to a piece of flying metal. A lad, Ernest Darnall of Bremond, was perched in a tree for a good view of the “battle.” He was mortally wounded, as was DeWitt Barnew of Hewitt, who was almost decapitated by a flying wedge of metal. One woman riding in a wagon half a mile away was hit by a flying hunk of lumber and knocked unconscious. In all, two people were killed, nine were seriously injured, and many others received minor injuries.

Willie Crush was immediately fired, but he was soon rehired and given the task of paying those who were injured. Despite the disastrous outcome of the event, He did make the Katy a household word. A Texas Musician named Scott Joplin even wrote a piano solo which he named “The Great Crush Collision March.” For many years, a historical marker stood at the bottom of the two sloping hills that marked the short-lived town of Crush, Texas. Over time, the sign was removed to make way for a main highway that runs alongside the town of West, Texas. Many drivers venture down I-35 daily, never realizing the catastrophe that once occurred beneath their tires.
